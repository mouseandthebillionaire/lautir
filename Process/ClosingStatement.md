
# Closing Statement

It turns out it's _especially_ difficult to make a contemplative game about time. Or, rather, it is difficult to make a game that forces a modern audience to engage with slow time. To wait. To sit. To be at rest. Byung-Chul Han highlights this difficulty in _The Scent of Time_.

_"Today's temporal crisis is caused by desynchronicity which leads to various temporal disturbances and irritations. Time is lacking a rhythm that would provide order and thus it falls out of step. Desynchronicity lets time whizz, so to speak. The feeling that life is accelerating is really the experience of a time that is whizzing without a direction . . . Nothing compares time. Life is no longer embedded in any ordering structures or coordinates that were found duration even things with which we identify are fleeting and ephemeral. Thus, we become radically transient ourselves."_ (vi)

The ways we have structured the world, our lives, even our _modes of relaxation_, are hurried. It is more important to fit a lot in than it is to enjoy the slow moment. More important to accumulate than to foster. Coffee breaks have turned into networking opportunities or times to catch up on our socials, rather than true respites. The point of playing a game becomes to get to the end rather than slowly enjoy the challenges.

It should be noted that this slowness isn't paramount to the contemplative experience. It isn't the only way to be in the world. The one solution to a happy and fulfilled life. Much as the _vita contemplativa_ is the foil to the _vita activa_, this slowed, watchful time works on cooperation with the general speed of life. It brings to mind Andrew Forbes' notion that baseball is "a contemplative tedium interrupted by bursts of significant action." (2016, 5)

Is there a way to make a _thoughtful_ game that holds both these truths? Are most games already doing that? Does the bench in _Hollow Knight_ or the napping spots in _Stray_ fill this need? Is it an actual rest? Or does a truly contemplative game _by itself_ fulfill the role in an already timeless, hurried, and thoughtless time?

That was the goal here. To force a moment of calm. To force an opportunity to sit and wait for a few minutes, do a simple task (enter a word), and be rewarded with something (hopefully) beautiful and thought-provoking. But that ask... That ask of time, of sitting there _for an undetermined amount of time_... Many people will find that difficult. 

_"Give me audio feedback when it's noon."_ -BLWM

In the modern standards of digital experiences, especially web-based experiences, both users and designers are hoping for as _little_ friction as possible. We want to make the experience as smooth and easy as we can. But isn't that ease part of the problem? When we are trying to instigate contemplation, might we need to rip the user _out_ of time? Out of flow? I don't think that this same prejudice exists for other media, at least not to this level. Movies can be slow. We can't pause the theatre or skip the parts we're uninterested in. Albums can be challenging at times (though this feels less and less true for contemporary pop?) But a game that makes you wait? Currently, I am playing the _Castlevania: Symphony of Shadows_ port for the PS4(5) and both the saving screens and the time it takes to get back into the game post-death/game-over feel horrendously wrong. They feel _insulting_. 

But what if we _can_ convince a player to wait? If we can get their buy-in that what we are supposing is beneficial. In other words, is this particular play experiment successful? Generally, I would say yes. As long as players are willing to engage in this way, it works. The convincing is a bit of a hill to overcome, as I have mentioned, but once over that hill, if the time is spent in focused attention, we can foster the contemplative spirit.

_"this number of sound layers is where there's a shift from focusing on the semantic meaning to the sound layering. Same components essentially but now a refocused attention - now I want to listen to each layer and I care less about the words. Very nice how this shift happens."_ -RK

It should be noted that semantic meaning is something that I hadn't considered too deeply. As someone who has only a cursory interest in song lyrics and cares more about how the _sound_ of voice interacts with the other instruments, this makes sense. But clearly this is something to be thinking about. If we are asking the user to enter words there will be inherent meaning tied to their choices. It might be too difficult to thematically tie semantic meaning to musical sound, but how can we still honor those choices?
